Lesson eight. From small talk to real talk.

Small talk is a doorway. In this lesson we walk through it.

Not every conversation needs to go deep. A pleasant two minutes with a neighbor is complete as it is. But sometimes you meet a person you would like to know better. A possible friend. A colleague you will work beside for years. Someone you find yourself drawn to. Then the question is how to move from the weather to something that matters, without lurching.

Think of conversation as having three levels.

The first level is facts. Where you live, what you do, how long you have been here. It is safe, and a little dry.

The second level is opinions and preferences. What you think of the city. Which job you liked best. Whether you are a morning person. This is where personality starts to show.

The third level is feelings and meaning. What you love about your work. What was hard about moving. What you are hoping for. This is where people begin to actually know each other.

Most stalled conversations are stuck on level one. Two people trading facts like business cards. The art is to step down one level at a time. And the steps are simply better questions.

To move from facts to opinions, ask what they think. You have lived here ten years. What do you make of how it has changed? You have done both jobs. Which suited you better?

To move from opinions to meaning, ask about why, and about feeling. A small warning here. The word "why" on its own can sound like a challenge. Why did you become a nurse? Soften it. What drew you to nursing? What do you love about it? What was that like for you? What is the hardest part?

Listen to it happen.

A: So how long have you been teaching?
B: Twelve years now. All at the same school.
A: Twelve years. What has kept you there?
B: Honestly? There is a moment, maybe twice a year, when a kid who has been struggling suddenly gets it. You can see it happen on their face. I think I stay for that.

Three sentences, and you are no longer making small talk. You are hearing what a person lives for. And all it took was one question about meaning, asked with real interest.

The second way to go deeper is to go first. Remember the trade of disclosures. People generally match the depth you offer. If you stay on the surface, so will they. If you offer something a little more real, they will usually follow.

A: I moved here two years ago, and I will be honest, the first year was lonelier than I expected. How did you find it when you arrived?

That is not a confession. It is a small, true thing, one step below the surface. It tells the other person that this is a conversation where true things are welcome. Take one step, not five. Then see whether they take a step toward you.

Because depth must be invited, never forced. Watch how the person responds. If they answer a deeper question with energy and detail, they are glad you asked. If they go brief, change the subject, or look away, they are telling you gently that this is far enough for today. Then return to lighter ground at once, with no fuss. Lightness is always safe to go back to. A good conversationalist is like a good dance partner. You suggest the next step. You never drag.

A few questions that open the deeper levels kindly, once some warmth is there. What are you excited about at the moment? What is the best thing that has happened to you this year? What would you do if you had a whole free month? What did you want to be when you were a child? How did the two of you meet? These are still light. But each is a small door into something real.

Finding common ground is the other great deepener. When you discover you share something, a home town, a hobby, a taste in terrible films, an experience of raising teenagers, say so, and stay there a while. Me too are two of the most bonding words in any language. Only be careful that it does not turn into the shift response. Share the connection, then hand the conversation back. You are from Portland? I lived there for three years. Which part of town were you in?

And then the final step, the one most people leave out. If you have enjoyed someone, give the connection a future. Do not rely on chance to bring you together again. Say it plainly.

A: I have really enjoyed talking with you. I would love to continue this. Could I get your number? Or: are you ever free for a coffee?

Or more lightly: I will send you the name of that book. What is the best way to reach you? A specific reason to follow up makes the asking easy.

And then do it. Send the message within a day or two, and mention something from your conversation. Friendships and partnerships are not built in one conversation. They are built by the second one, and the third. The person who follows up is the person who ends up with a full life.

Your exercise. In one conversation this week, with someone you already know slightly, ask one question from the third level. What do you love about it? What was that like? Then be quiet, and listen to what comes.
