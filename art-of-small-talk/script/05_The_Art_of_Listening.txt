Lesson five. The art of listening.

Here is the secret that separates good conversationalists from everyone else. They are not thinking about what to say next. They are listening. And the strange result is that, because they are listening, they always have something to say next.

Let me show you why.

When people answer a question, they almost never give only what was asked. They add extra. I call these extras free information. Suppose you ask someone how their weekend was, and they say:

B: Pretty quiet. We just got back from visiting my sister in Denver, so I mostly did laundry and recovered.

You asked one question. You received at least four threads. A sister. Denver. Travel. And the word "we", which means there is someone else in the picture. Any of those is your next question. How was Denver? Do you get to see your sister often? You never have to invent a topic. The other person keeps handing them to you. You only have to catch them.

The anxious talker misses all of it. While the other person is speaking, the anxious talker is busy composing a clever next line, and so hears nothing. Then the answer ends, and there is nothing to work with, and the panic gets worse. The cure is almost funny in its simplicity. Stop preparing. Listen to the answer. The answer contains your next line.

Now, the single most valuable move in conversation: the follow up question.

Researchers at Harvard studied thousands of conversations, including speed dates, and found that people who ask more questions are better liked. And the kind of question that mattered most was the follow up. A question about the thing the person just said. Why? Because a follow up is proof. Anyone can ask "how was your weekend". Only someone who was listening can ask "and did your sister like the new house?"

A good habit is to follow up at least twice before you change the subject or talk about yourself. One question is politeness. The second and third are interest.

Next, a distinction that will change how you hear every conversation for the rest of your life. A sociologist named Charles Derber noticed that when someone says something, you can respond in one of two ways. He called them the shift response and the support response.

Suppose a colleague says: I am exhausted. I barely slept last night.

The shift response moves the attention to you.

A: Oh, me too. I have not slept properly in weeks. My neighbor has started learning the drums.

The support response keeps the attention on them.

A: Oh no. What kept you up?

Neither is a crime. But listen for it, and you will notice that many people shift every single time. Whatever you say reminds them of something about themselves. Talking with them feels like tossing a ball to someone who puts it in their pocket. The skilled conversationalist gives two or three support responses first. Then, when they do share their own story, it arrives as company, not competition.

There are smaller listening tools as well.

The echo. Repeat the last few words, or the key words, as a gentle question. I just got back from Peru. Peru? It sounds almost too simple, but it works beautifully. It says, go on, without steering.

The encourager. Small sounds and words. Really? And then what happened? No. You are joking. These are the fuel that keeps a storyteller going.

The reflection. Now and then, say back the feeling you heard. That sounds exhausting. You must have been thrilled. When you name the feeling correctly, people light up. They feel understood, which is rarer than it should be.

And then there is listening with your body. Face the person. Keep your eyes mostly on theirs, looking away now and then so it is not a stare. Nod. Let your face react to what they say. And put the phone away. Not face down on the table. Away. A phone on the table tells the other person that they are being compared, moment by moment, with everything else in the world.

A related discipline: do not interrupt, and do not finish people's sentences. Give a half second of silence after they stop. Often they will add one more thing, and that last thing is frequently the most interesting.

Finally, remember what you hear. Nothing makes a deeper impression than being remembered. When you meet someone again and ask, how did your daughter's recital go? You have told them that they mattered enough to keep. If your memory is unreliable, write a note after you part. Their name, and one thing they told you. There is nothing cold about that. It is how thoughtful people have always managed.

Your exercise. In your next three conversations, your only goal is to ask two follow up questions before saying anything about yourself. Afterward, notice how the other person responded, and notice how much easier it was than you expected.
