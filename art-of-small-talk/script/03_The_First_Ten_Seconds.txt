Lesson three. The first ten seconds.

The hardest part of any conversation is the part before it exists. So this lesson is about openers.

Let me take away a burden straight away. There are no magic opening lines. Clever openers are for films. In real life, the content of your first sentence matters very little. What matters is that it is friendly, easy to answer, and delivered as though you expect it to go well. An ordinary remark, said warmly, beats a brilliant one said nervously, every time.

Before any words, there is the approach. Three things. Eye contact, for a second or two. A smile that reaches your eyes. And an open body, shoulders turned toward the person, hands visible, phone away. People decide whether to welcome you before you finish your first word. Let your face say hello before your mouth does.

Now the words. Nearly every good opener comes from one of three places. I call it the triangle: the setting, the other person, and yourself.

The setting is the safest corner. You and the other person share a place and a moment, so comment on it, or ask about it.

A: This line has not moved in five minutes. Do you think they are roasting the beans by hand back there?
B: I was just thinking that. I am one more minute from giving up coffee forever.

At a conference: what did you think of that last talk? At a wedding: how do you know the couple? At a dog park: what is his name? In a new office: I am still learning where everything is. Is there a good place for lunch around here? The setting is rich with openers, because you are both already inside it.

The second corner is the other person. Notice something about them that they chose, and say something kind or curious about it.

A: That is a great bag. Can I ask where it is from?
B: Oh, thank you. A little shop in Lisbon, actually. I carried it home on the plane like a baby.

Notice the rule there. Compliment choices, not bodies. The book they are reading. The bicycle. The presentation they gave. The remarkable sandwich. A compliment on a choice invites a story. And attach a question to the compliment, so they have something to do with it besides saying thanks.

The third corner is you. Offer a small, honest observation about your own situation.

A: I have to admit I do not know a single person here. I am Maya, by the way.
B: Ha. Then that makes two of us. I am Daniel.

That one deserves special attention, because it is the most underrated opener there is. A plain statement of mild vulnerability, followed by your name. It works because it is true for half the room.

Which brings us to the simplest opener of all. Hello, I am, and then your name. Said with a smile and, where it suits the culture, an offered hand. In any setting where people have gathered on purpose, a party, a class, a meeting, this is completely sufficient. Nobody has ever been offended by a friendly introduction.

Three small rules for openers.

One. Prefer something positive, or at least playful. Opening with a complaint can work, as the coffee line showed, but only if it is light and shared. A real grumble makes people step back.

Two. Ask for a little, not a lot. A request for a small opinion or a bit of help is wonderful. Do you know if this train stops at Central? Which of these would you choose? People like being useful, and a small favor makes them feel warmly toward the person they helped.

Three. Do not wait. There is a moment, about three seconds long, when speaking to someone is natural. You both reach for the same door. You both laugh at the same announcement. If you hesitate, the moment closes, and the same sentence becomes harder to say. When you feel the impulse, count three, two, one, and speak.

What about the weather? Use it without shame. The weather is the one topic every human being shares, and it has opened more friendships than any other subject on earth. The trick is to treat it as a first step and not a destination. Cold today, and then: are you someone who likes the winter? Now you are talking about the person, not the sky.

And if the opener gets nothing back? A one word answer, a polite nod, eyes returning to the phone? Then smile, and let it go. You have lost nothing, and you have read the signal correctly. Most of the time, though, you will get a sentence back. And a sentence is all you need, because the next lesson will show you what to do with it.

Your exercise. Tomorrow, open three conversations using the triangle. One from the setting, one from the other person, one from yourself. They can each last ten seconds. You are practicing the start, nothing more.
