Lesson seven. Keeping it moving.

You have opened. You have asked. You have listened and given something back. Two minutes have passed, and now comes the moment everyone dreads. The topic runs dry. Both of you nod. Someone says, so. Yeah.

This lesson is about that moment, and how to make sure it never frightens you again.

First, change what you believe about silence. A pause in a conversation is not a failure. It is punctuation. Watch two old friends talk, and you will see comfortable gaps all the time. The gap only becomes awkward when someone panics. If you stay relaxed, take a breath, take a sip of your drink, smile, the pause simply passes. Three seconds feels like a year inside your head. From the outside, it looks like a person thinking.

Now, the tools.

Tool one: threading. You met this idea in the lesson on listening, and here it becomes a method. Every sentence a person speaks contains several threads. Your job is to pull one. Suppose someone says:

B: I just moved here from Boston for a new job at the hospital.

Count the threads. Moving. Here, this city. Boston. A new job. The hospital. Five, in one sentence. You could say: how are you finding it here so far? Or: what do you miss about Boston? Or: what is the new job? Any one will do. And the answer to that question will contain four or five more.

Tool two: the saved thread. This is what turns a dry spell into nothing at all. When someone gives you five threads, you can only pull one. Keep the others in your pocket. Then, when the current topic fades, reach back.

A: You mentioned earlier that you moved from Boston. What made you choose here?

This does two things at once. It restarts the conversation, and it proves you were listening minutes ago. People are quietly delighted by it. So while you listen, notice the threads you are not pulling, and keep one or two in reserve.

Tool three: the bridge. Sometimes you want to change the subject entirely. You do not need a clever link. You need only a short phrase that announces the turn. That reminds me. Speaking of which. Completely different subject, but. Oh, I meant to ask you. Any of these gives you permission to go anywhere. People change topics abruptly all the time in friendly talk. Announcing it is all the grace required.

Tool four: return to the setting. The place you are in keeps producing material. The food arrives. The music changes. Someone gives a speech. A dog does something ridiculous. Have you tried these? What do you think of this place? When everything else fails, look around.

Tool five: the reliable standbys. Keep a few questions that work on nearly anyone. Have you got any trips coming up? Have you seen or read anything good lately? Are you working on anything fun at the moment? What do you do when you are not working? And, at the right season: any plans for the holidays? These are not original. They do not need to be. They are bridges to whatever the person actually cares about.

Tool six: play. Not every exchange needs to be information. Some of the best small talk is a little game. A light, absurd question. If you could have dinner tonight from any restaurant in the world, where would it come from? What is the worst job you ever had? This sort of thing is not for the first thirty seconds, and it is not for every person. But once someone has smiled at something, a playful question often brings out more of them than ten sensible ones.

Now, the art of matching. People differ in pace, in energy, and in how much they want to say. A good conversationalist adjusts. If the other person is quiet and thoughtful, slow down and soften your voice. If they are lively and quick, bring more energy. If they give long, generous answers, you may ask bigger questions. If they give short ones, keep things light and offer more of yourself. This is not imitation. It is courtesy. You are meeting them where they are.

And then there is finding the spark. In almost every conversation, there comes a moment when the other person's face changes. Their eyes widen. They speak faster. They lean in. You have touched something they love. It might be their children, their motorcycle, their sourdough, a team, a place. When you see the spark, stay there. Put down whatever you were going to say and ask more. Tell me about that. How did you get into it? What is the best part? For the next five minutes, you need do nothing but enjoy their enthusiasm. They will remember you as a wonderful person to talk to.

What if they light no spark and offer no threads? Sometimes a person gives one word answers no matter what you try. Offer a little more of yourself, try two different territories, and if nothing catches, release them kindly. It is not a verdict on you. They may be shy, or tired, or worried about something far away. Lesson ten will show you how to leave with grace.

Your exercise. In your next conversation, silently count the threads in one of the other person's sentences. Pull one, and save one. Later, bring the saved one back with the words "you mentioned earlier". Watch their face when you do.
