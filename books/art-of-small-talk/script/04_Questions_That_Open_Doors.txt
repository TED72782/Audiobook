Lesson four. Questions that open doors.

If small talk has an engine, it is the question. But not all questions are equal. Some open a door. Some close it. Some feel like an interview under a bright lamp. This lesson is about telling them apart.

Start with the simplest distinction. A closed question can be answered in one word. Did you have a good weekend? Yes. Do you live nearby? No. An open question needs a sentence. What did you get up to this weekend? What brought you to this part of town?

Closed questions are not bad. They are easy to answer, which makes them a gentle way to begin. The mistake is to ask five of them in a row. That is how a conversation turns into a customs inspection. Where are you from. What do you do. How long have you been there. Each answer is a full stop.

So here is the basic rhythm. Start closed, then open. Do you work nearby? Yes, just around the corner. Oh, what sort of work is it?

Now a set of question types that reliably open doors.

The story question. In place of asking for a fact, ask for the story behind the fact. Not: where are you from? But: how did you end up in this city? Not: what do you do? But: how did you get into that line of work? The words "how did you" are a small key that fits almost any lock. A fact takes three words to give. A story takes a minute, and it hands you a dozen new things to ask about.

The highlight question. Ask for the best part. What has been the best thing about your week? What was the highlight of the trip? What are you enjoying most about the new job? This steers the person toward something they will enjoy talking about. People associate you with the feeling of the topic you raised. Raise good ones.

The looking forward question. What are you looking forward to this summer? Anything good planned for the weekend? Questions about the future are light, hopeful, and nearly impossible to get wrong.

The opinion question. Ask for a small recommendation or judgment. I am looking for something new to watch. Have you seen anything good? You know this area. Where would you take a visitor for dinner? This quietly tells the person that you value their taste.

The "what is that like" question. When someone mentions something outside your experience, a job you know nothing about, a town you have never visited, three small children, ask what it is like. What is it like, working nights? What is that like, having twins? It admits honest ignorance, and it invites them to be the expert. Almost everyone enjoys being the expert on their own life.

You may have heard of a memory aid called Ford. F, O, R, D. Family, occupation, recreation, dreams. Four territories that almost everyone can talk about. Family covers where people come from and who is in their life. Occupation is how they spend their days, paid or not. Recreation is what they do for pleasure. Dreams is what they hope for, the trip they want to take, the thing they would love to learn. When your mind goes blank, walk through Ford and pick a territory.

A caution about occupation. "What do you do?" is the most common question in many countries, and it is fine, but it lands badly on someone who is between jobs, or at home with children, or who simply dislikes their work. A kinder version is: what keeps you busy these days? It lets the person choose their own answer. They might say their job. They might say their garden.

Now the most important refinement in this lesson. A question should not arrive naked. If you only ask, and ask, and ask, the other person begins to feel examined. The cure is to wrap your question in a little statement first. Give a small piece of yourself, then ask.

Listen to the difference. Naked: where did you grow up? Wrapped: I grew up in a tiny town where everyone knew my business. Where did you grow up? The second version takes a tiny risk first. It shows what kind of answer you are hoping for. And it gives them something to respond to if they would rather not answer.

Here is how it sounds in practice.

A: I have been trying to cook more this year, with very mixed results. Are you much of a cook?
B: I am a one dish person. But it is a good dish. A chicken thing my grandmother used to make.
A: A grandmother recipe. Those are the best kind. What makes hers special?

Three sentences in, and they are talking about a grandmother. That is the door opening.

One last rule. Ask questions you actually want answered. Curiosity cannot be faked for long, and it does not need to be. Every person you meet knows something you do not, has been somewhere you have not, and cares about something you have never considered. Your job is to find out what.

Your exercise. Take three flat questions you often ask, and rewrite each one as a story question, a highlight question, or a wrapped question. Then use all three this week.
