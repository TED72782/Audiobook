Lesson ten. The graceful exit.

A surprising number of people avoid starting conversations because they do not know how to end them. They fear being trapped. Or they fear that leaving will seem rude, so they stay until the talk has withered and both people are searching the room with their eyes.

So let us make endings easy. When you know you can leave any conversation, smoothly and kindly, in fifteen seconds, you become much braver about starting them.

First, some permission. Ending a conversation is not a rejection. At a party or an event, everyone understands that people move around. Small talk is supposed to be short. Five or ten minutes is a full and proper conversation. The other person, very likely, also wants to move on, and is waiting for one of you to say so.

And here is a principle from the theater. Leave them wanting more. The best moment to end is not when the conversation is dying. It is just after a high point. After a good laugh, after a story has landed, while you are both still enjoying it. People remember how an exchange ended. End on warmth, and the whole conversation is remembered as warm.

A good exit has three parts. A signal. An appreciation. And a next step.

The signal tells them that the end is coming, so it is not abrupt. Often one word does it. Well. Anyway. Listen. Your voice lifts a little, and your body begins to turn.

The appreciation is the heart of it. Say one true, specific thing about the conversation.

The next step says where you are going or what happens next. It does not have to be elaborate, and it must be true.

Here is the whole shape.

A: Well, I am going to go and say hello to a few people before the evening gets away from me. But I have really enjoyed this. Good luck with the marathon training. I will be thinking of you at five in the morning.
B: Ha. Thank you. It was great to meet you.

Hear what happened there. The signal: well. The next step: I am going to say hello to a few people. The appreciation: I have enjoyed this. And then a small callback to something they said, the marathon, which shows one last time that you listened. That callback is the mark of a master. It takes five words, and it leaves a glow.

Some honest next steps you may use. I am going to get some food before it disappears. I should find my friend. I promised I would not abandon her. I need to catch someone before they leave. I should let you get back to your evening. I am going to mingle a little. At work: I had better get back to it. On the telephone: I will let you go. All plain. All true. No one has ever been hurt by them.

Do not invent an excuse. You do not need a false emergency, and false excuses tend to show. The plain truth, that you are going to move along now, is entirely acceptable when it is wrapped in appreciation.

There are also exits that take the other person with you. I am heading for the food. Would you like to come? Or the handoff. Have you met Rachel? Rachel, this is Omar. He has just been telling me about beekeeping. Once they are talking, you may slip away, and you have done both of them a service.

When you want to see the person again, the exit is the time to say so. Let us continue this. Are you on email? And then exchange details before you part.

Now the difficult cases.

The monologuer. Some people speak without pause, and there seems to be no opening. You will have to make one. Wait for a breath. Say their name, which stops almost anyone for a second. Then go straight to your exit, warmly and firmly. Michael, I have to stop you there, because I need to find my colleague before she leaves. But this has been fascinating. Enjoy the rest of your evening. Then actually go. Interrupting kindly is not rude. Standing there resenting someone for twenty minutes is far less kind.

The conversation that never started. If it lasted thirty seconds and went nowhere, the exit is tiny. Well, it was nice to meet you. Enjoy the party. Smile, and go.

The person who follows your exit with a new subject. Repeat your exit once, more briefly, already moving. I really must go. Lovely to meet you.

And the place you cannot leave, the airplane seat, the long dinner table. Here, you end the conversation without ending the nearness. Well, I am going to get back to my book. But it has been a pleasure talking with you. Then open the book. Headphones, in that situation, are a universal and perfectly polite signal.

One final touch. When you leave, leave completely. Hold eye contact as you say goodbye. Use their name if you have it. Smile. Then turn and walk with purpose. A lingering half exit, where you say goodbye and then hover, undoes all your good work.

Your exercise. Memorize your own three part exit, in your own words. Then practice ending two conversations this week while they are still going well. It will feel early. That feeling is what "leaving them wanting more" feels like from the inside.
