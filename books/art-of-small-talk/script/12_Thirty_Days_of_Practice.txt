Lesson twelve. Thirty days of practice.

You have now heard everything I have to teach you about small talk. And if you stop here, almost none of it will stay.

That is not an insult. It is how skills work. Understanding a thing and being able to do it are stored in different places. The only bridge between them is repetition. So this final lesson is a plan. Thirty days, four weeks, each with one focus. A few minutes a day.

Week one. Warm up.

Your only task this week is to become someone who greets people. Each day, give a real hello, with eye contact and a smile, to three people you would normally pass in silence. The neighbor. The person at the front desk. The other parent at the school gate. And with one of them each day, add a single sentence. A comment about the day, a small compliment, a question. That is all.

By the end of the week you will have made twenty one greetings and seven tiny conversations. More importantly, you will have taught your nervous system, by direct experience, that nothing bad happens.

Week two. Open and ask.

This week, start one real conversation a day. Two or three minutes. Use the triangle for your opener: the setting, the other person, or yourself. Then practice your questions. Open questions. Story questions. How did you get into that? And remember to wrap your questions with a little of yourself. Each evening, take ten seconds to notice which opener you used and how it went. Simply to notice, not to judge.

Week three. Listen and give.

Keep the daily conversation, and change what you attend to. This week it is all about the middle. Listen for free information. Ask two follow up questions before you talk about yourself. Catch yourself when you are about to shift the attention to you, and give a support response first. Use your new answers to "how are you" and "what do you do". Leave hooks. And once a day, save a thread, and bring it back with "you mentioned earlier".

Week four. Stretch.

Now put it all together, and reach a little further. Go to one gathering this week. Something at work, a class, a community event, a party. Arrive early. Speak to someone standing alone. Join one open group. Learn three names, and use each one three times. Take one conversation a level deeper by asking what someone loves about what they do. Practice a three part exit while things are going well. And with one person you enjoyed, follow up within two days.

That is the plan. A few notes on how to carry it out.

Keep the stakes low. Practice first where nothing depends on it. Shops, queues, waiting rooms, dog parks. By the time you are in a conversation that matters to you, the moves should be nearly automatic.

Expect some duds. In thirty days, a handful of your attempts will fall flat. Someone will not look up. A joke will miss. Count each one as a success, because the task was to try, and you tried. Musicians practice with wrong notes. That is what practice is.

Keep score lightly. A small note on your phone, with a mark for each conversation, is strangely motivating. Not a record of how well they went. Only that they happened.

And review once a week. Ask yourself two questions. What worked? And what will I try next time? That is all the analysis you need.

Before we finish, let me gather the whole course into a few sentences you can carry with you.

Small talk is not about the words. It is a way of telling another person: I see you, and you are welcome here.

You do not have to be interesting. You have to be interested.

Go first. Most people are waiting, and hoping, for someone else to begin.

Open with what is around you. Ask for stories, not facts. Listen to the answer, because the answer holds your next question.

Follow up. Support before you shift. Remember what you are told.

Give something back, with a hook in it.

When it goes quiet, breathe. Pull a thread, or reach for one you saved.

Go deeper one step at a time, and only by invitation.

In a room, look for the person standing alone.

Leave while it is still good, with a word of thanks.

And through all of it, be kind. Technique in the service of kindness is charm. Technique without it is only manipulation, and people can tell the difference.

Let me leave you with one last thought. Every person you will ever love, work with, learn from, or be helped by is, at this moment, either someone you already know or a stranger. And the only way a stranger has ever become anything else is that somebody said something small. Nice day. Is this seat taken? How do you know the host?

Most of those little sentences lead nowhere in particular. A pleasant minute, and goodbye. That is already worth having. But now and then, one of them turns out to be the first line of a friendship, or a career, or a marriage. You can never tell in advance which one it will be. You can only keep saying the small things, warmly, to the people in front of you.

So the next time you are standing in that kitchen, or waiting for that meeting, and the quiet voice says, say something, you know what to do.

Smile. Go first. Be curious.

Thank you for listening. Now go and talk to someone.
