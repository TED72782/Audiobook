Lesson nine. Working the room.

Talking with one person is one skill. Walking into a room of forty people who all seem to know each other is another. This lesson is about groups: how to enter, how to join, how to remember names, and how to bring others in.

Start before you arrive. Decide on a small, clear goal. Not "be charming". Something you can count. I will have three real conversations. I will meet two people I do not know. A small goal turns a vague ordeal into a task, and tasks are far less frightening. It also gives you permission to leave once it is done.

Arrive early if you can. This is the opposite of what nervous people do, and it is one of the best pieces of advice I can give you. Early in an event, the room is small. People are standing alone or in pairs. Everyone is glad to be spoken to. Arrive late, and the groups have already formed and the noise is high. The early guest is helping the room begin. The late guest has to break in.

When you enter, pause for a moment and look around. You are looking for the easiest places to start. There are three.

The first is anyone standing alone. That person is hoping someone will come over. Be that someone. You will rarely get a warmer welcome.

The second is wherever people naturally wait. The food, the drinks, the coat rack, the line for anything. People standing in line have nothing to do, and a shared subject is right in front of them.

The third is the host, or anyone you already know. Greet them, and let them introduce you onward. You can even ask: is there someone here you think I should meet?

Now, joining a group. First read its shape. Two people standing face to face, close together, speaking low, are in a private conversation. Leave them. But a group standing in a loose curve, with a gap, bodies angled outward, is open. That gap is an invitation.

Walk up to the gap. Stand at the edge. Listen for a few moments, so you know what is being discussed, and catch someone's eye and smile. Usually the circle will shift a little to let you in. Then, at a natural pause, either add a brief comment on the topic, or simply say: hello, I am Sam. Mind if I join you?

Nobody says no. And do not change the subject the moment you arrive. Join the conversation that exists before you start a new one.

Next, names. People care about their names more than they will admit, and remembering one is a small act of respect. Yet most of us forget a name within seconds of hearing it. The reason is simple. We were not listening. At the moment of introduction, we are thinking about our own handshake and our own name.

So here is a method in four parts.

Hear it. For that one second, pay attention to nothing else. If you miss it, ask at once. Sorry, I did not catch your name. That is no insult. It shows you care to get it right.

Say it. Use it immediately. Good to meet you, Priya.

Tie it to something. Privately connect the name with their face or with something they tell you. Priya, who paints. Priya, with the green scarf.

Use it again. Once in the middle of the conversation, and once when you part. It was lovely to meet you, Priya. Three uses, and it will usually stay.

And when you have forgotten, which everyone does? Confess quickly and warmly.

A: I am so sorry. I remember our whole conversation about your trip to Japan, but your name has completely left me.

That works because it shows that you remember them, which is what matters. If you are with a friend, you have another way out. Introduce your friend. Have you two met? This is my friend Tom. The other person will almost always answer with their own name.

Now the skill that marks a true master: including others.

When someone approaches your group, open the circle. Step back half a pace, turn your body, and bring them up to date. We were just talking about the worst flights we have ever been on. Do you have one?

When you introduce two people, give each a hook. Not only their names. Ana, this is David. David has just come back from cycling across Spain. David, Ana is the one who told me about that bakery. Now they have something to say to each other, and you may quietly move on.

And watch for the quiet one. In every group there is someone who has not spoken for a while. Turn to them with an easy question. Maria, you used to live there, did you not? What did you think of it? To be drawn into a circle by name is a kindness people remember for a very long time.

A room is not something to conquer. It is a collection of individuals, most of them a little unsure, all of them hoping for a friendly face. You can be that face. Do it one person at a time.

Your exercise. At your next gathering, of any size, do two things. Speak to one person who is standing alone. And introduce two people to each other, with a hook for each.
