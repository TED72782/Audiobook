Lesson six. Giving something back.

So far I have urged you to ask and to listen. Now a correction, so you do not take it too far. A conversation in which one person only asks questions is not a conversation. It is an interview. And the person being interviewed walks away thinking: that was pleasant, but I know nothing about them.

Good small talk is a game of catch. They throw, you throw. Trust between people is built by a trade of small disclosures. You tell me a little, I tell you a little, and each exchange goes one step further. If you never take your turn, the trade stops.

So this lesson is about your half. How to answer well, and how to offer something of yourself.

Begin with the two questions you will be asked more than any others. How are you? And: what do you do? Most people throw both away.

How are you? Fine, thanks. And there the conversation dies, with nothing to hold.

The fix is simple. Answer with one real detail. Not your medical history. One small, specific thing, preferably cheerful.

B: I am good, thanks. I finally fixed a tap that has been dripping for a month, so I am feeling unreasonably proud of myself. How about you?

Hear what that does. It is honest. It is brief. It has a little humor. And it hands the other person three things to pick up: home repairs, small victories, pride. Then it returns the question. One detail, then hand it back. That is the whole formula.

Now, what do you do? The usual answer is a job title. I am an accountant. A title is a label, and a label is a closed door, unless the other person happens to know your field. Give them a picture in its place. Say what you actually spend your days doing, or who you help, or the part you like.

B: I am an accountant. Mostly for small restaurants. So I spend a lot of time telling very talented chefs that they cannot afford another oven.

Now there is something to ask about. Restaurants. Chefs. Ovens. The same works for any answer. Not just: I am from Ohio. But: I am from a small town in Ohio, known mainly for a very large statue of a basket. Not just: I went hiking. But: I went hiking, and was passed on the trail by a woman who must have been eighty.

Here is the principle. Leave hooks. A hook is a specific, concrete detail that someone can grab. Flat answers are smooth. There is nothing to hold. Good answers have texture. Every time you answer a question, add one hook. You are making the other person's job easy, which is the kindest thing a conversationalist can do.

How long should you speak? In small talk, a good turn is short. Twenty or thirty seconds. Long enough to give something real, short enough that they never feel trapped. If you notice you have been speaking for a full minute, land the plane. Finish your sentence, and pass the ball: anyway, what about you?

It helps a great deal to keep a few things ready. I do not mean a script. I mean a small pocketful of answers to the question, what have you been up to? Something you watched or read lately. A place you went. A project you are in the middle of. Something small that made you laugh this week. Before a party or an event, take one minute to think of three. Then when someone asks what is new, you will not say "not much". Never say "not much". Something is always new.

A word on what to share. In early conversation, aim for what I would call the warm middle. Not so private that the other person does not know what to do with it. Not so bland that it reveals nothing. A mild frustration. A small enthusiasm. An honest opinion about something that does not matter very much. I have strong feelings about how to load a dishwasher. That kind of thing.

And a word on what to hold back at the start. Heavy troubles. Strong positions on politics and religion with people you have just met. Complaints about people who are not present. Anything that sounds like bragging. None of these is forbidden forever. They belong to later, once there is trust to carry them.

What about humor? You do not need jokes. Prepared jokes are risky. What works is lightness. A willingness to find things a little funny, especially yourself. Gentle humor at your own expense, about small things, makes people comfortable. Humor at someone else's expense does the opposite. Be careful, though, that you are laughing at your small mishaps, not running yourself down. "I got lost twice on the way here" is charming. "I am hopeless at everything" makes people uneasy.

Last, enthusiasm. Allow yourself to like things out loud. Many of us learned at some point to be cool, which means showing no delight in anything. But delight is magnetic. The person who says, with real feeling, "I have just discovered how to make bread, and I cannot stop", is a pleasure to stand next to.

Your exercise. Write down, right now if you can, a better answer to "how are you" and a better answer to "what do you do". One sentence each, with one hook each. Use them the next time you are asked. You will be asked today.
