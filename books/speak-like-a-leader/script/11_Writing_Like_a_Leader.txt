Lesson eleven. Writing like a leader.

These days, most leadership communication is written. Emails, chat messages, updates, short documents. Written words travel further than spoken ones. They get forwarded, quoted, and reread, sometimes months later. And they lack the tone of voice and facial expression that soften spoken words. So it pays to write with care.

The good news is that everything you have learned so far applies. Say the point first. Be concrete. Know your one message. Writing simply makes these habits more visible.

Start with the subject line or the first line, because that is often all people read. Make it say what the message is for. Not, quick question, or, update. Instead: Decision needed by Thursday: new supplier. Or: Launch moved to March fifteenth, no action needed. The reader should know, before opening the message, whether it matters to them and what it asks of them.

Then the first sentence. Put the request, decision, or key fact there. Can you approve the attached budget by Thursday? Or: We've decided to move the launch to March, and here's why. Everything after that first sentence is support, and some readers will never get to it. Make sure the ones who stop early still got the point.

Keep it short. A long email is usually a sign that the writer has not yet worked out what they think. Try this: after writing a message, cut it by a third. You will almost always find that it improves. Short paragraphs, a few lines each. If you have several points, number them, so people can reply to point two without rewriting everything.

Be explicit about what you need, from whom, and by when. One of the most common causes of wasted time in organizations is the message that ends without a clear ask. Everyone reads it, nobody acts, because nobody is sure it was meant for them. Name people. Sam, can you check the numbers in section two by Wednesday? Priya, no action needed, just keeping you informed.

Mind your tone. Written words often read colder than you meant them. A short message that felt efficient to you, Fine. Do it, may read as anger to the person receiving it. When something matters, read your message once as the other person will read it, especially if they are junior, worried, or under pressure. A single warm sentence at the start often makes all the difference. Thanks for pulling this together so quickly.

Know when not to write. Some messages should never be sent in writing. Bad news that affects someone personally. Serious criticism. Anything emotional, or any topic where misunderstanding could cause real harm. Those conversations deserve a voice and, ideally, a face. A good rule: praise in writing, so people can keep it and share it. Deliver hard news in person, then follow up in writing with the facts.

Never write when angry. If a message makes you furious, write your reply if you must, but do not send it. Save it, and look at it the next morning. You will almost always be glad you waited. A message sent in anger can be read and forwarded forever. A message delayed overnight costs you nothing.

Remember that a leader's message is never private. Assume that anything you write could be forwarded to the person you are writing about, or read aloud in a meeting a year from now. If you would be embarrassed by that, rewrite it.

Finally, the written update. Many leaders send a short regular note to their team, weekly or monthly. It is one of the simplest and most effective habits in leadership. Keep it brief, predictable, and human. What happened. What's coming. One thing to celebrate. One thing you need. Over time, these notes become a record of the team's story, and a reliable reason for everyone to feel informed rather than left in the dark.

Your exercise. Before sending your next three important emails, check each one against four questions. Does the subject line say what it's for? Is the main point in the first sentence? Is the ask clear, with a name and a date? Could it be a third shorter? Fix whatever fails, then send.
