Lesson twelve. Thirty days of practice.

You have now heard everything this course has to teach. And, as with any skill, very little of it will stick unless you practice. So this final lesson is a plan. Four weeks, each with one focus. A few minutes a day, built into the work you are already doing.

Week one. Clarity and presence.

This week, focus on how you say things. Before every important message, written or spoken, finish the sentence: the one thing I need them to understand is. Put that point first. In conversations, slow down a little, finish your sentences firmly, and practice one deliberate pause before your most important point. At the end of each day, ask yourself: where was I clearest today, and where did I ramble?

Week two. Listening.

This week, practice speaking last in meetings. Ask at least one real question a day, a question you genuinely do not know the answer to. When someone brings you a problem, respond first with: thank you for telling me. Summarize what people say before giving your own view. And close one loop: tell someone what happened to an idea or concern they raised.

Week three. Feedback and hard conversations.

This week, give at least three pieces of specific praise, using situation, behavior, and impact. Give one piece of constructive feedback, privately and promptly. Ask one person for feedback about yourself, and respond only with thanks and a question. And have one conversation you have been putting off. Prepare the core message in two sentences, say it early, and then let the silence work.

Week four. The wider stage.

This week, put it all together. State the purpose at the start of every meeting you run, and end with the three questions: what did we decide, who will do what, by when. Tell one true story about your team at its best. Send a short written update to your team. And with one person whose support you need, have a conversation where your only goal is to understand what they care about.

A few notes on how to make this work.

Keep a small log. A few lines each day: what I tried, what happened, what I'll try next. It takes two minutes and turns experience into learning.

Ask for help. Tell a trusted colleague what you are practicing, and ask them to tell you what they notice. A second pair of eyes speeds up any skill.

Expect awkward moments. New habits feel clumsy before they feel natural. That is not failure. That is what learning feels like from the inside.

And be patient with yourself. You will not do all of this perfectly, and you do not need to. Leadership communication is not a performance you get right once. It is a practice you return to every day.

Before we finish, let me gather the whole course into a few sentences you can carry with you.

A leader's words exist to create clarity, confidence, and connection.

Your manner speaks before your words do. Be calm, steady, and consistent.

Say the point first. Use plain words. Explain why, not just what.

Listen more than you talk. Speak last. Thank people for bad news.

Connect the daily work to a purpose, and tell true stories that show it.

Give feedback on behavior, specifically and promptly. Ask for it yourself.

Say hard things early and plainly, then let the silence work.

Give every meeting a purpose, and end it with decisions, owners, and dates.

In uncertain times, say what you know, what you don't, and when you'll update people. Then keep that promise.

Influence by understanding what others care about, and never by manipulation.

Write short, clear messages with a clear ask, and never send one in anger.

And through all of it, remember that people will forget much of what you say. But they will remember whether you were honest with them, whether you listened, and whether you made them feel part of something worth doing.

That is what it means to communicate like a leader.

Thank you for listening. Now go and lead.
