Lesson four. Listening like a leader.

Here is a strange truth about leadership. The more senior you become, the less people tell you. Not because they have less to say, but because speaking up to someone with power over you feels risky. Bad news gets softened. Doubts go unspoken. Good ideas die in people's heads because the moment never felt safe.

So one of the most important communication skills a leader has is not speaking at all. It is listening in a way that makes people want to speak.

Researchers have a name for the condition that makes this possible. The Harvard professor Amy Edmondson calls it psychological safety: a shared belief within a team that it is safe to take interpersonal risks. Safe to ask a question, admit a mistake, raise a concern, or disagree with the boss, without being embarrassed or punished. Her research found that the best teams were not the ones that made the fewest errors. They were the ones that reported their errors, because they felt safe enough to do so. And when Google studied its own teams in a project called Aristotle, psychological safety emerged as the most important factor separating the most effective teams from the rest.

Psychological safety is built, or destroyed, mostly by how a leader listens. Here is how to build it.

First, listen before you speak. In meetings, try speaking last rather than first. The moment a leader states an opinion, the conversation narrows. People start agreeing, or quietly decide not to bother disagreeing. If you ask for views first, you will hear what people actually think, which is the whole point of asking.

Second, ask real questions. Not questions that are really instructions in disguise, like, don't you think we should do it my way? Real questions, where you do not know the answer. What are we missing? What would you do if you were in my seat? What is the thing nobody is saying? One of the most useful questions a leader can ask is simply: what's getting in your way?

Third, respond well to bad news. This is the moment of truth. When someone brings you a problem, your first reaction teaches them whether to do it again. If you sigh, frown, or snap, they will learn to hide problems. If you thank them, even when the news is unwelcome, they will learn to bring problems early.

A: Can I flag something? I think we're going to miss the deadline on the Henderson project.

B: Thank you for telling me now, while we can still do something about it. Walk me through what's happening.

That first sentence, thank you for telling me, may be the most valuable sentence in leadership. It costs nothing, and it keeps the truth flowing toward you.

Fourth, summarize what you heard. Before you respond with your own view, play back the other person's. So the main concern is that the new process adds a day to every order, and customers will notice. Have I got that right? This does three things. It proves you listened. It catches misunderstandings early. And it gives the other person a chance to add the thing they were too nervous to say the first time.

Fifth, listen for what is underneath. People often lead with a small, safe version of their real concern. Someone who asks about a detail of the new schedule may really be worried about whether their job is changing. When you sense something more, invite it gently. It sounds like there might be more to this. What's on your mind?

Sixth, close the loop. Nothing kills honest feedback faster than asking for it and then doing nothing visible. When someone raises an idea or a concern, tell them what happened to it, even if the answer is no. Especially if the answer is no. We looked at your suggestion about the rota. We're not changing it this quarter, because of the holiday cover problem, but it's on the list for spring. People can accept a no. What they cannot accept is silence.

A word on the hardest listening situation of all: being criticized. When someone tells you that you made a mistake, your instinct will be to explain, justify, or defend. Resist it, at least at first. Say, tell me more. Ask for an example. Then thank them. You can decide later whether you agree. But if you argue every piece of feedback, you will soon stop receiving any.

Your exercise. In your next meeting, speak last. Ask one real question to which you do not know the answer, and when the answer comes, summarize it back before you add anything of your own. Afterward, notice whether people said more than usual.
