Lesson nine. Leading through change and uncertainty.

The real test of a leader's communication comes when things are uncertain. A reorganization, a difficult year, a sudden crisis, a change nobody asked for. In those moments, people are anxious, rumors move fast, and every word from the top is examined closely.

The most common mistake leaders make in uncertain times is going quiet. They do not want to say anything until they have all the answers. It feels responsible. But silence is not neutral. When leaders say nothing, people fill the gap themselves, usually with the worst possible story. And by the time the official answer arrives, the rumor has already won.

So the first principle is: communicate early, even when you do not have all the answers. Here is a simple and very useful structure for uncertain moments. Say what you know. Say what you do not know yet. And say when you will next update people.

A: Here's where things stand. We know the company is merging our team with the Leeds office. We don't yet know what that means for individual roles, and I won't guess, because I don't want to tell you something that turns out to be wrong. I'll have more by the twentieth, and I'll update you that day, even if the update is that we're still waiting.

That message does not solve the uncertainty. But it does three valuable things. It shows honesty. It sets expectations. And it gives people a date to hold onto, which reduces the need to fill the silence with speculation. Then, and this is crucial, keep the promise. Update on the twentieth, even if there is little news. Reliability builds the trust you will need later.

The second principle: be honest about the hard parts. Leaders often try to reassure people by sounding more positive than the facts allow. People see through it immediately, and it costs credibility. It is far better to say, this is going to be a hard few months, and here is how we'll get through it. People can handle difficult truths. What they cannot handle is the feeling that they are being managed.

There is a balance here, and it is an old one. Be realistic about the present, and hopeful about the future. Name the difficulty honestly, and in the same breath, give people something they can do and something to believe in. Hope that ignores reality is naive. Reality without hope is crushing. Leaders hold both.

The third principle: repeat yourself, much more than feels necessary. The leadership writer John Kotter argued that leaders under communicate a change vision by a factor of ten. By the time you are tired of saying something, people are just beginning to hear it. In times of change, people are distracted, worried, and filtering everything through their own concerns. Say the important message in meetings, in writing, one to one, and in passing. Say it in slightly different words each time, but keep the core identical.

The fourth principle: talk about what is not changing. Change makes people feel the ground is moving under them. Naming what stays the same is enormously steadying. Our customers stay the same. Our standards stay the same. This team stays together. Give people something solid to stand on.

The fifth principle: answer the question people are really asking. In any change, the first question in everyone's mind is, what does this mean for me? Will my job change? Will my boss change? Will I have to move? Until that question is addressed, people will not hear anything about strategy or vision. Address the personal question as directly as you honestly can, and then talk about the bigger picture.

And in a true crisis, when something has gone badly wrong and people are frightened, keep your communication short, frequent, and calm. Lead with the most important fact. Tell people exactly what to do. Avoid speculation. Show that someone is in charge and paying attention. Your steadiness, which we talked about in lesson two, matters more in a crisis than at any other time.

Finally, listen during change, more than ever. Hold open sessions. Ask, what are you hearing? What worries you most? You will learn which rumors are circulating, which questions you have not answered, and where people need more support. Change communication is not a broadcast. It is a conversation that lasts as long as the change does.

Your exercise. Think of something uncertain your team is facing right now, large or small. Write a short update using the three part structure: what we know, what we don't know yet, and when you'll hear from me next. Then share it.
