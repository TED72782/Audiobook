Lesson three. Clarity: say the point first.

If I could give leaders only one piece of communication advice, it would be this. Say the point first.

Most of us were taught to communicate like a mystery novel. We set the scene, introduce the details, build the reasoning, and arrive at the conclusion at the end. That works in a story. In leadership, it wastes people's attention. By the time you reach your point, half the room has drifted, and the other half is trying to guess where you are going.

Military writers have a name for the alternative: bottom line up front. State the conclusion, the decision, or the request in your first sentence. Then give the reasons. Then the details, for anyone who needs them.

Listen to the difference.

A: So, I've been looking at the numbers from the last quarter, and there were some interesting things in the regional data, especially in the north, and after talking to a few people, I started to wonder whether...

B: I recommend we pause the northern expansion for three months. Two reasons. Sales there are below plan, and we don't yet have a manager in place. I'll walk you through the numbers.

The second version can be interrupted at any point and still be useful. If the meeting ends after one sentence, everyone knows the recommendation. That is clarity.

The second rule of clarity: decide what your one message is. Before any important conversation, email, or presentation, try to finish this sentence: The one thing I need them to understand is. If you cannot finish that sentence, you are not ready to communicate yet. You are still thinking. That is fine, but do your thinking first, privately, rather than out loud at everyone else's expense.

The third rule: use plain words. Leaders often slip into a strange dialect, full of words like leverage, alignment, synergies, and bandwidth. These words feel professional, but they blur meaning. Ask what they actually stand for. Leverage our capabilities might mean use what we're good at. Align on priorities might mean agree on what comes first. When in doubt, say it the way you would explain it to a smart friend over dinner.

The fourth rule: be concrete. Abstract statements slide off the mind. Specific ones stick. Customer service needs to improve is abstract. We want every customer email answered within one working day is concrete. Now people know what success looks like, and can tell when they have achieved it.

The fifth rule: use structure people can hold. The mind likes small numbers. There are three reasons. We have two options. Here is the one risk that worries me. A number at the start acts like a map: listeners know how much is coming, and they can follow along. Three is a particularly useful number. It feels complete without being overwhelming.

The sixth rule is subtle, and it is one of the most powerful ideas in leadership. Explain your intent, not just your instructions. Armies learned long ago that detailed plans rarely survive contact with reality. Conditions change, and the people on the ground must adapt. So good military orders include what is called the commander's intent: the purpose behind the plan, and what success looks like. If the plan breaks down, people can still act in the spirit of it.

The same is true in any team. Compare these two instructions.

A: Call every customer on this list by Friday.

B: Call every customer on this list by Friday. The goal is that nobody hears about the price change from someone else first. If you can't reach someone by phone, an email is fine, as long as it's personal.

The second version tells people what matters, so they can make good decisions when the plan meets reality. Explaining why also shows respect. It treats people as partners who think, not as hands that follow.

Finally, check for understanding. Never ask, does that make sense? People almost always say yes, out of politeness or embarrassment. Instead, ask them to play it back. Just so I know I explained it well, what will you do first? Notice the wording. It puts the responsibility on your explanation, not on their intelligence. If what comes back is different from what you meant, the fault is in the message, and now you can fix it.

Your exercise. Take the next important email you need to send. Before writing anything else, write the one sentence you most need the reader to understand, and make it the first line. Then add no more than three short reasons. Cut everything that doesn't serve that first sentence.
