Lesson eight. Meetings, and speaking to a room.

Meetings are where a great deal of leadership communication actually happens, and where a great deal of time is lost. A leader who runs good meetings gives people back hours of their week, and earns respect that no speech can buy.

The first rule of a good meeting is to know why it exists. Before calling one, finish this sentence: by the end of this meeting, we will have. Decided which option to choose. Agreed who does what. Understood the new process. If you cannot finish the sentence, you probably need an email, not a meeting.

Then say that purpose out loud at the start. The aim today is to choose between the two suppliers. We have thirty minutes. This takes ten seconds and transforms the meeting, because everyone now knows what success looks like, and can help steer toward it.

During the meeting, the leader's job is mostly to facilitate, not to perform. Draw out quiet people by name. Mei, you know the suppliers best. What's your view? Gently stop people who dominate. Thanks, James, I want to hear from a few others before we go further. Park side topics instead of chasing them. That's important, and I'll make sure we pick it up separately.

Remember the advice from lesson four. If you want honest views, give yours last. And if the topic is sensitive, give people a moment to think, or even write their views down, before anyone speaks. That stops the first loud opinion from setting the course for everyone else.

The end of a meeting is where most meetings fail. Ideas were discussed, everyone nodded, and then nobody is quite sure what was decided. Reserve the last five minutes for three questions. What did we decide? Who will do what? By when? Say the answers out loud, and then send them in writing the same day. That one habit will make you more effective than almost anything else in this course.

Now, speaking to a larger room. A team briefing, a presentation, a talk at an all hands meeting. Many capable leaders dread these. Let me offer a few principles that make them far easier.

Prepare a message, not a script. Know the one thing you want people to remember, the three points that support it, and the story that brings it to life. Then speak from those notes, rather than reading. Reading aloud puts a wall between you and the room. Speaking from a few notes lets you look at people, and lets your real voice come through.

Open strong. Do not open with an apology, a technical problem, or a list of thank yous. Open with something that earns attention: a question, a surprising fact, a short story, or your main point stated plainly. You get the room's full attention for the first minute. Use it.

Slow down, and pause. Everything from lesson two applies, with extra force. Nerves make speakers rush, and a room needs more time than a single listener to absorb an idea. A pause after your main point gives it time to land.

Use slides to help the audience, not to hold your notes. A slide should be readable in about three seconds: a picture, a chart, a few words. If a slide is full of sentences, people will read it instead of listening to you, and they will read faster than you speak.

Prepare for questions. Think of the three hardest questions someone could ask, and how you would answer them. When a question comes, listen to the end, pause, and answer the question that was asked, briefly. If you do not know, say so: I don't know, and I'll find out and come back to you by Friday. Then do it. That answer builds more trust than any bluff.

Finish with your main point, again. The ending is what people remember, so do not let your talk trail off with, so, yeah, that's it. Close with your one message, and, if appropriate, what you are asking people to do.

As for nerves, they never fully go away, even for experienced speakers, and they do not need to. A little adrenaline makes you sharper. Breathe out slowly before you begin. Look for a few friendly faces. And remember that the audience wants you to succeed. Nobody sits in a room hoping the speaker will fail.

Your exercise. At the start of your next meeting, state its purpose in one sentence. At the end, ask the three closing questions out loud: what did we decide, who will do what, and by when. Send the answers in writing that afternoon.
