Lesson seven. Hard conversations.

Every leader eventually has to say things people do not want to hear. A project is cancelled. A promotion went to someone else. Someone's work is not good enough. A colleague's behavior is hurting the team. These conversations are uncomfortable, and so they are often delayed, softened into mush, or avoided altogether.

But avoiding a hard conversation does not make the problem go away. It makes it grow. And the person on the other side usually knows something is wrong, so the silence becomes its own kind of message. Handling hard conversations well is one of the clearest marks of a trustworthy leader.

Start with preparation. Before the conversation, answer three questions for yourself. What exactly is the message? What outcome do I want? And how might they feel, and why? Write the message down in one or two sentences. Under pressure, people drift away from their point, and a written sentence keeps you anchored.

Then, in the conversation itself, say the hard thing early. Many people circle for ten minutes, making small talk while the other person grows anxious, sensing that something is coming. That is not kindness. Kindness is clarity. Give a short signal, then deliver the message plainly.

A: I have some difficult news, and I want to be straight with you. We've decided not to move forward with your proposal this year.

Then stop talking. This is the hardest part. After a hard message, there is a silence, and every instinct will push you to fill it with explanations, softeners, and reassurance. Let the silence be. The other person needs a moment to take in what you said. If you rush to fill it, you will often undo your own message.

Next, acknowledge the feeling. Not to argue with it, and not to fix it. Just to name it. I can see this is disappointing. I know how much work you put into it. People can accept a hard decision far more easily when they feel their reaction has been seen.

B: Honestly, I'm frustrated. I thought the numbers were strong.

A: That's fair, and the numbers were strong. Can I explain how we decided?

Notice that the leader does not say, don't be frustrated, or, you shouldn't feel that way. They accept the feeling, and offer an explanation, as a question. Explaining your reasoning matters. Even when people disagree with a decision, they find it much easier to accept when they understand how it was made, and believe the process was fair.

Be careful to separate what is decided from what is open. If the decision is final, say so clearly. False hope is crueler than a clear no. If something is still open, say that too, and invite input on it. The proposal won't go ahead this year. What is open is how we use the work you've done, and I'd like your ideas on that.

Now, a different kind of hard conversation: disagreement. Sometimes you will need to disagree with your own boss, with a powerful colleague, or with a room that has already made up its mind. A few habits help.

Disagree with the idea, not the person. Instead of, you're wrong, try, I see it differently, and here is why.

Start with what you agree on. We both want the launch to succeed. Where we differ is on timing.

Be curious about their view. Help me understand what you're seeing that I'm not. Sometimes you will discover they are right.

And once a decision is made, if it is not unethical, commit to it fully. Leaders who disagree in the room and then undermine the decision in the hallway destroy trust in every direction. The phrase to remember is: disagree and commit.

Finally, the hardest conversation of all: admitting your own mistake. Leaders sometimes fear that admitting error will weaken their authority. In practice, it strengthens it, as long as it is done well. Be specific about what you got wrong. Take responsibility without excuses. Say what you will do differently. I made the wrong call on the supplier. I moved too fast and didn't ask the people closest to it. Here's what we're doing to fix it. Short, honest, and forward looking. People forgive mistakes far more easily than they forgive cover ups.

Your exercise. Think of a hard conversation you have been putting off. Write the core message in two sentences, and write the first sentence you will say out loud. Then pick a day to have it, this week.
