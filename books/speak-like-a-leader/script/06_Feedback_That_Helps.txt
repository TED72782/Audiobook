Lesson six. Feedback that helps.

Feedback is where many leaders struggle most. Some avoid it, hoping problems will fix themselves. Others give it so bluntly that people stop listening. And many give only vague praise, like great job, which feels nice but teaches nothing.

Good feedback has a simple aim. Not to judge someone, and not to relieve the leader's frustration. Its aim is to help the person do better work next time. Keep that aim in mind, and most of the right choices follow.

Let us start with a tool that makes feedback clearer and fairer. It is often called the situation, behavior, impact model. Describe the specific situation. Describe the behavior you observed, as a fact, not an interpretation. Then describe its impact.

Compare these.

A: You're always so negative in meetings.

B: In Monday's planning meeting, when Priya proposed the new schedule, you said it would never work, before she'd finished explaining it. I noticed she didn't say anything else for the rest of the meeting. I'm worried we lost her ideas.

The first is a judgement about character. It invites denial, because nobody is always anything. The second is a description of one moment, one action, and one consequence. It is much harder to argue with, and much easier to act on.

Notice the word behavior. Give feedback on what people do, not who they are. You were late to three client calls this month, not you're unreliable. Behaviors can change. Labels just hurt.

Then, after describing the impact, stop and ask. How did you see it? There may be something you don't know. Perhaps Priya's schedule really does have a fatal flaw. Perhaps there's a reason for the late calls. Asking turns feedback from a verdict into a conversation, and conversations are where change actually happens.

Finish by looking forward. What would you like to see next time? Or better, ask them. What would you do differently? A plan people make themselves is one they are far more likely to follow.

Now, timing. Feedback is most useful when it is close to the event, while everyone still remembers the details. Waiting months for a formal review means the person has repeated the problem dozens of times, and then feels ambushed. Small, frequent feedback is kinder than rare, large feedback. And private is almost always better for criticism. Praise in public, correct in private, is old advice because it is good advice.

Praise deserves just as much care as criticism, and gets far less. Great job is a pat on the head. Specific praise is a lesson. Compare: nice presentation, with this: the way you opened with the customer's complaint got everyone's attention immediately, and your summary slide made the decision easy. Keep doing that. Now the person knows exactly what to repeat. Specific praise is one of the most powerful tools a leader has, and it costs nothing.

A common technique you may have heard of is the feedback sandwich: a compliment, then the criticism, then another compliment. It is well intentioned, but people quickly learn the pattern, and start bracing for the bad news every time they are praised. It is usually better to keep them separate. Give praise because it is deserved, and give criticism because it is useful. Each will be heard more clearly on its own.

Some leaders worry about being too harsh. Others worry about being too soft. The writer Kim Scott describes the sweet spot well. She calls it caring personally while challenging directly. Care without challenge becomes vague niceness that lets people fail. Challenge without care becomes cruelty that people resist. Do both, and people will hear hard things because they know you are on their side.

Finally, the most powerful way to make feedback normal is to ask for it yourself. Ask your team: what's one thing I could do to make your job easier? What should I stop doing? Then use what they told you from lesson four: thank them, don't argue, and act on something visible. When people see the leader receive feedback gracefully, giving and receiving it stops feeling dangerous for everyone.

Your exercise. This week, give two pieces of specific praise, using situation, behavior, and impact. Then ask one person on your team for one piece of feedback about yourself, and respond only with thank you, and one clarifying question.
